# COMPUTE CORE — concept round

Five concepts, three sizes each · **status: concepts drawn, nothing selected**

Sheets: `compute_cores.png` (all five), and `core_a.png` … `core_e.png` one row
each at full size. Builders: `tools/concepts_core.gd`. Renderer:
`tools/shoot_cores.gd`.

---

## 0. What this round is actually deciding

The compute core is the capture objective. It ships at three sizes and all
three are **the same form scaled**:

| scene | measured W x H x L |
|---|---|
| `compute_core_small.tscn` | 1.88 x 4.19 x 1.88 |
| `compute_core.tscn` | 3.38 x 6.97 x 3.38 |
| `compute_core_large.tscn` | 5.19 x 12.38 x 5.19 |

Those three numbers came out of the renderer, not out of the brief, and the
photograph that goes with them is the argument: rendered side by side through
the icon studio, which fits every cell to its own bounds, **the three are
indistinguishable**. That is not a quirk of the studio. It is what the player
sees, because perspective does the same thing the studio does: a 12.4 m core at
60 m and a 4.2 m core at 20 m subtend the same angle and present the same
shape, and nothing in the silhouette breaks the tie.

So the question is not "which of these is prettiest". It is:

> **Should the three sizes be one form scaled, or three different forms?**

One form scaled says *the same thing, more of it* — which is true, cheap, and
the reason it was built that way. Three forms says *this is a bigger prize and
a longer fight* — which is the judgement the player has to make on approach and
currently cannot.

**A and B argue one form. C, D and E argue three.** Each section below says
which position it takes, what the position buys, what it costs, and the honest
weakness. §6 ranks them.

### Three things that constrain every answer

**This is architecture, not a robot.** The shipped cores are TrenchBroom brush
geometry, generated by `core_column()` in `tools/block_industrial.gd` and owned
by the TERRAIN lane. Everything on these sheets is drawn with CSG primitives
**only because CSG is what the icon studio can photograph** — no part of
`concept_kit.gd`'s frame vocabulary is used, there is no head, no chamfered
hull and no limb anywhere in `concepts_core.gd`. Read every box as a brush.
Where a cylinder appears it stands for a 12-sided brush drum, which is what
`compute_chiller_yard` already uses.

**The player walks up to it and presses a key.** `InteractRaycast` is 2 m long,
masks the Interactible layer only, and does not fire when it *starts* inside
the volume — so reach is the core's own footprint and not a metre more
(`Env/world_objects/compute_core_point.tscn` carries the full note). Every
concept here therefore has an **approach face**: a recess at x = 0 on the -Z
side with a lit plate between y 1.1 and 2.0 and a kick rail under it, built by
the same `_console()` helper in all fifteen builders. A form with no
approachable face is disqualified however good it looks, and on the sheets the
Walker is stood at that face to prove there is one.

**The Walker on the sheets is a real ruler.** It is `concept_kit`'s reference
build, measured at 3.99 m in kit units and scaled x0.955 to the 3.81 m the
Walker actually ships at. Each row is **one photograph with one camera**, so
within a row the proportions are true. Rows do not share a scale with each
other — the Walker is the only thing that carries across rows, and that is what
it is there for.

## 1. A — CASSETTE STACK

**Position: ONE FORM, SCALED.** Measured 2.17 x 4.20 x 2.22 · 3.89 x 7.00 x
3.97 · 5.71 x 12.40 x 5.79.

The shipped core, restated with exactly one rule added: **the cassette is a
constant 1.0 m at every size**. Today the module scales with the column, so the
small core is three cassettes of 1.0 m and the large is five of 2.1 m, and the
three photograph identically. Hold the module and the small core is a stack of
three, the medium five, the large nine. Everything else is `core_column()`
verbatim — stepped plinth, collar, lit slot under every cassette, service
alcove in the bottom one, battered cage, risers up the back, sheared head.

**What it buys.** It is the cheapest answer on the sheet by a wide margin: one
builder, one collision profile, one nav footprint, three integer arguments. It
keeps the family absolutely intact, because it *is* the family. And a count is
something the eye reads without a reference object in frame — nine bands and
three bands are different pictures in a way that "tall" and "short" are not.

**What it costs.** Nothing structural. The large is 5.71 m across against the
shipped 5.19, because the cage posts now stand proud; the reach volumes in
`compute_core_point*.tscn` would need re-measuring, which is a number change
and not a design change.

**The honest weakness, and it is real.** *Counting is a slow read.* At the
range where the judgement matters — spotting the objective across a map — nine
stripes and three stripes are both "a striped column", and the louvre ribs on
the flanks compete with the module joints for the same horizontal lines. Look
at `core_a.png` with the Walker covered up: the three are still obviously
different, but they are different the way three sizes of the same book are
different, and you have to already know the module is 1 m for the count to mean
anything. **It makes the existing form measurable. It does not make it
legible.** That is less than the brief asked for and the sheet does not pretend
otherwise.

Rejected inside this concept: varying the cassette *proportion* with size (a
tall thin small one, a squat wide large one). It reads as three badly
proportioned versions of one object rather than as a deliberate family, and it
throws away the only thing the scaled position has going for it.

## 2. B — MAST AND DESK

**Position: ONE FORM, SCALED — with a ruler bolted on.** Measured 2.32 x 4.20 x
3.57 · 3.34 x 7.00 x 4.38 · 5.07 x 12.40 x 5.76.

A battered mast in `compute_monolith`'s language — three stages at 93% of each
other, a groove up the front face so they read as one mast and not three boxes,
and a top metre sheared off at 34 degrees with the lit plate lying in the cut.
In front of it, **a console that never changes size**: a 1.6 m desk with a
sloped working face, a hood at 2.3 m on two posts, a handrail and a cable tray,
built once at robot scale and bolted to all three.

The argument is that a scaled form is only unreadable because there is nothing
in it of known size. Put a known size *in* the silhouette and the same shape at
three heights reads as three heights.

**What it buys.** Two things, and the second is the better one. First, a
built-in ruler: the desk is a Walker-and-a-bit wide at 4.2 m and a detail at
12.4 m, and that ratio is the size cue. Second, and this is a gameplay win the
other four do not offer — **the approach face is in the same place, at the same
size, on all three sizes.** One `reach_size`, one `channel_range`, one
indicator height, one answer to "where do I stand". Today those are three
different numbers in three scenes because the footprint scales.

**What it costs.** The desk is authored furniture that has to be brushed once
and then not touched, and it adds about 2 m of depth in front of the mast that
level designers have to leave clear — a core in a corridor now needs a bay.

**The honest weakness.** *The small one is mostly desk.* At 4.2 m the console
assembly is over half the object's visual mass (look at the left-hand group in
`core_b.png`), and the thing reads as a bus shelter with a pillar behind it.
The opposite failure appears at 12.4 m, where the desk is a detail you cannot
see past 40 m — and a constant-size detail under perspective reads as **"far
away", not as "big"**, which is the exact confusion the concept was meant to
remove. The ruler only works when the ground plane is visible too.

Two earlier versions of this are worth recording because they failed in ways
that are easy to repeat. A 16%-per-stage taper photographed as a wedding cake —
three boxes, not a mast. And a shear cut with a rotated *box* only knocks a
corner off: it took a true half-space cut (`_shear_top()`) to get a flat
slanted top, which is why that helper exists and is commented.

## 3. C — UNIT / MACHINE / INSTALLATION

**Position: THREE FORMS.** Measured 2.40 x 4.20 x 2.00 · 6.10 x 7.00 x 3.00 ·
13.00 x 12.57 x 15.27.

Three different things that happen to do the same job.

- **Small is one cabinet.** A fridge with a hatch, louvres down both flanks,
  lifting eyes on the lid and a drum on top, bolted to a pad. 1.9 m across, so
  it drops straight into the slot the shipped small core occupies. It looks
  like something a Reclaimer could winch away.
- **Medium is a machine.** Two cabinets with a 1.2 m working slot between them
  — room for one robot and not for a squad — under a gantry whose posts are the
  7.0 m, carrying the trays and a chiller on the deck. Too big to move, too
  small to garrison.
- **Large is an installation.** A 13 x 9 m deck at 1.2 m with a ramp up the
  front, four cabinet columns standing on it, a roof truss over them at 10 m,
  drums along the back edge and a stair tower in the back corner making the
  12.4. You walk *onto* it.

**What it buys.** Exactly the judgement the brief says is missing, and it buys
it at any range, in any lighting, from any angle: one object, a machine, a
building. Nothing about that read depends on the player knowing the module size
or seeing the ground. The ramp is also the only thing on any of these sheets
that makes the objective *terrain* — the squad fights on it, which is a
genuinely different holding fight and the thing a 12.4 m prize ought to feel
like.

**What it costs.** Three builds, three collision profiles, three nav bakes, and
the deck is a walkable surface, which means it has to be baked as one — note
that `_ramp()` exists because `move_and_slide` has no step-up and a lip of any
height stops a body dead, the same note `yard_slab()` carries in
`block_industrial.gd`. The TERRAIN lane authors three sets of brushes instead
of one parametric column.

**The honest weakness.** *The large one stops being a core and starts being a
data hall.* `compute_data_hall` already exists in the kit and is a long low
building with a plinth; at 13 x 15 m with a roof and a stair, C-large is
competing with it for the same read, and on a map that has both, the player has
to tell "the objective" from "the scenery" by something other than shape.
That is survivable — the lit faces and the ramp are the tell — but it is a real
cost and it is the reason this concept is not an automatic win.

Rejected: making the large a *row* of the small cabinet, nine of them in a yard.
It reads as inventory rather than as an installation, and a repeated unit has
the same slow-count problem as A with none of A's cheapness.

## 4. D — BLOCK / SLOT / COURT

**Position: THREE FORMS.** Measured 3.22 x 4.20 x 3.01 · 8.00 x 7.00 x 2.00 ·
15.20 x 12.40 x 13.72.

The only concept here where what changes with size is not mass but **enclosure
— where the player has to put his body to press the key.**

- **Small is a block.** A sealed 2.2 m slab with a chamfered crown, one lit
  recess on the front face, bollards round the base. You stand in the open
  beside it.
- **Medium is a slot.** Two slabs 2.4 m apart under a lintel, consoles facing
  each other on the inner walls. You step into a doorway to take it.
- **Large is a court.** Six slabs on a ring of 6.4 m with the front and back
  bays left open, an overhead ring tying the heads, and a drum altar in the
  middle carrying the console. You go *inside*, and while you hold it you
  cannot see out.

**What it buys.** The size changes the fight, not just the look. A big core
costs more because of where it makes you stand: the court is a killing box with
two doors, the slot is a one-robot doorway you can be pinned in, the block is a
thing you lean round. That is a real mechanical difference obtained purely from
geometry, with no code anywhere. It is also the most striking of the five on
the page by some distance.

**What it costs.** The most of any concept, and the costs are the kind that
only show up in play. The court needs a nav bake that lets a squad inside and
round the altar; the slot is a 2.4 m corridor that two bodies will fight over;
the interiors are cover geometry nobody has balanced. The medium's consoles sit
on faces pointing along +/-X rather than -Z, so it is the one concept whose
approach face is not where every other objective in the game puts it.

**The honest weakness.** *The large one's best idea is invisible from outside.*
The whole point of the court is what it feels like inside it, and from 50 m
away it is a ring of slabs — tall, impressive, and no more informative about
"how long am I stuck here" than the thing it replaces. It also fails the
brief's reach rule in the obvious way if it is ever built with the console on
an outer face, and it is the concept most likely to trap a squad against its
own geometry. **High ceiling, low floor.** If it works it is the best thing
here; if it does not, it fails in play rather than on the page, which is the
expensive place to find out.

## 5. E — PYLON AND APRON

**Position: THREE FORMS, by what piles up underneath.** Measured 2.80 x 4.23 x
2.80 · 7.00 x 7.06 x 7.00 · 16.00 x 12.49 x 16.00.

One tall element — a braced heat pylon, legs battered in, X-bracing, a cassette
head — reads the same at all three sizes *on purpose*: it is the landmark you
steer by and it should be recognisable at a kilometre. What changes is the
**apron**.

- **Small** is the pylon on a 2.8 m pad, with the lit cassette slung between
  the front legs at 1.5 m as the approach face.
- **Medium** adds a skirt: a 7 x 7 apron, drums at the feet, a transformer, a
  handrail, the console on a box at the base.
- **Large** stands the pylon on a two-storey 10 m block with a 3.6 m approach
  bay cut into the front of it, inside a 16 m apron with four drums, trays and
  rails.

So the footprint goes **2.8 → 7 → 16 m while the height goes 4.2 → 7 → 12.4**.

**What it buys.** The best far-read on the sheet, for a reason that is
geometric rather than aesthetic: **perspective flattens height, it does not
flatten ground.** A tall thin thing at 200 m is a tall thin thing whatever its
height; a 16 m apron is still visibly a long walk across open ground before you
can read a single detail on it. The player's judgement — *how long will I be
stuck here* — is literally answered by how much cleared ground he has to cross,
which is also the thing that decides the fight. And the large's approach bay
puts the player under five metres of concrete to press the key: the most
defensible spot on the map and a box he can be grenaded in, which is a nice
tension to hand a designer for free.

**What it costs.** The apron is a large flat claim on the map. A core that
needs 16 x 16 m of clear ground cannot go in a street, a trench or a building
interior, and three of the shipped kit pieces assume it can. It is the least
placeable of the five.

**The honest weakness.** *The small one is spindly and does not read as a
prize.* At 4.2 m it is an open lattice with a box in it — closer to a
surveyor's tower than to anything worth capturing, and the lit cassette doing
the work of saying "objective" is 1.7 m of the 2.8 m frame. There is also a
family risk: a braced lattice is not currently in the compute kit's vocabulary
at all (the kit is slabs, plinths, drums and trays), so E is the one concept
that adds a new material language rather than recombining the existing one.

Recorded because it cost a render: the pylon legs and the cage posts both
leaned the **wrong way** in the first pass and every one of them splayed out
like a derrick stood on its head. A rotation of *t* about Z takes +Y to
(-sin t, cos t), so the +X post needs **+t** to lean in; about X, the +Z post
needs **-t**. Both helpers now carry that note.

## 6. Ranking, and why

The brief asked for a recommendation, so here is one, with the reasoning
exposed so it can be disagreed with.

**1. C — UNIT / MACHINE / INSTALLATION.** It is the only concept that answers
the actual question — *how big a prize is this* — with no preconditions. It
does not need the ground plane visible (B does), it does not need the player to
count (A does), and its best idea is on the outside where it can be seen (D's
is not). The large one being walkable is a bonus that changes the holding fight
in the direction the fiction already wants: you take the installation, you do
not touch the obelisk. Its risk — colliding with `compute_data_hall` — is a
dressing problem and dressing problems are solvable.

**2. E — PYLON AND APRON.** Second because its far-read is the best of all five
and its argument about footprint-versus-height is the most rigorous thing on
this page. Second rather than first only because of placeability: a 16 m apron
rules the large core out of most of the maps that exist, and the small one does
not look like an objective. If the answer to "where do large cores go" is "in
open ground, always", E moves to first.

**3. A — CASSETTE STACK.** Third on merit, first on cost. If this round's
verdict is "not now", A is the change to make anyway: it is a one-line fix to
`core_column()`'s call sites, it breaks nothing, and it makes the existing trio
*measurable* even if it does not make it legible. It is the honest minimum.

**4. D — BLOCK / SLOT / COURT.** The most interesting idea here and the one I
would most like to be wrong about. It is fourth because its payoff is invisible
until someone plays it, its risks are nav and cover risks that this lane cannot
evaluate, and two of its three forms put the interact face somewhere no other
objective in the game puts it. **Worth a spike on one map, not a commitment.**

**5. B — MAST AND DESK.** Last, and the sheet is why. The fixed console is a
genuinely clever idea and it buys the one unambiguously good gameplay
property — identical reach at all three sizes — but it photographs as a bus
shelter at 4.2 m and as an invisible detail at 12.4, and a constant-size cue
under perspective says "far" at least as loudly as it says "big". It solves the
measurement problem without solving the legibility problem, which is what A
also does, for a fraction of the authoring.

**What I cannot judge, and the human can.** Whether a 12.4 m core should be
something you climb onto (C, and to a lesser extent D) or something you stand
at the foot of (A, B, E) is a feel question about the holding fight, and the
holding fight is the part of this I cannot play. If the answer is "the squad
should fight *on* the big one", C and D are the only two candidates and the
ranking above collapses to a choice between them.

## 7. Notes for whoever builds the selected one

- **Measure, do not estimate.** `tools/shoot_cores.gd` prints every builder's
  real bounding box and the shipped scenes' alongside, with subtraction shapes
  excluded. Every number in this document came out of that run.
- The renderer is a **sibling** of `tools/shoot_concepts.gd`, not an extension
  of it, and the reason is in its header: that script fits every cell to its
  own bounds, which normalises away the one property this round is about. A
  row here is one photograph with one camera.
- Nothing in this round touched `maps/`, any `.map`, `Env/world_objects/` or
  any objective script. The builders are a drawing of an intention; the brush
  work is the TERRAIN lane's.
- The sheets in this directory are documentation images, not game resources —
  they have no `.import` files, which is correct until someone opens the editor
  and does not matter either way.
